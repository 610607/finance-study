### The first shock, 1973–1974

- **frus1973oil d209**: war began 6 October 1973; at the first WSAG meeting (9 a.m., 6 October) Atherton put the chance of
  an embargo “very high”; Saudi Arabia “the key to the oil problem”; Rush: no plans, the United States would be “in a
  helluva fix”, as would Europe and Japan. Kissinger on 7 October: the Arabs “have to learn what the limits are or
  they will nibble us to death. But this is a helluva time to teach them the limits.”
- **frus1973oil d216**: 14 October Kissinger wrote Faisal that the airlift to Israel was “not intended as anti-Arab”;
  Yamani told the French Saudi Arabia would cut production by 5 percent per month over a year; Fahd (15 October): the
  relationship “can never be the same again”; Faisal (16 October) “pained”.
- **frus1973oil d220** (staff meeting, 18 October 1973, 12:10 p.m.): Sisco: “a price increase from $3 a barrel to a little
  over $5 a barrel”, the companies “have no alternative” but to go along; Kissinger: “All right, but the next year they
  go to $10. Will they then have to accept that too?” … “Then they go to $20. Is there some point at which they have
  to resist?”; Rush: coal at $5 becomes cheaper than oil for many things. A possible opening scene: the word
  “resist” is the chapter's policy question (accommodate or resist).
- **frus1973oil d223** (CIA, 19 October): on 17 October OAPEC decided to cut production “not less than 5% a month” until
  Israeli withdrawal and the Palestinians' “legal rights”; deliveries kept to “friendly” countries; the US receives
  only about 1.6 million b/d of Arab oil; Western Europe depends on Arab oil for 70% of its consumption, Japan for
  40%; “the brunt” falls on Europe and Japan; reserves about two months (France 90 days); the price rise adds about
  $3 billion each to the US and Japanese import bills and nearly $8 billion to Western Europe's.
- **bis1974** p. 3: the cut was 5% a month cumulatively from the September level, “or about 1 million barrels a day”;
  p. 4: about 2% of world output a month; early November changed to an immediate 25% cut on September, then the
  monthly cuts; an embargo on the United States and the Netherlands, while France and the United Kingdom were to
  receive their average of the first nine months of 1973; the posted price of light crude raised 70% “from $3.01 to
  $5.11 per barrel”, then on 23 December to $11.65 for three months from 1 January 1974; the producers' “take” went
  from $1.75 to $7 a barrel, the c.i.f. price in Europe from $3.65 to $8.90; oil imports were 70% of total energy
  supplies in Japan, about 60% in western Europe (twice the share of the early 1960s), 10% in the United States;
  two days after the December price rise output was restored to 85% of September; the US embargo lifted in late
  March; US car sales in March 1974 21% below September 1973. p. 5: the table “Oil imports in relation to GNP and
  trade, 1973” (net oil imports 0.5% of GNP in the US, 1.6% in Japan and Germany, 1.9% in the UK and Switzerland,
  2.2% in Denmark; 17.6% of Japan's imports); the direct effect on consumer prices 2–3% in most European countries,
  nearly 4% in Japan. p. 6: the importing countries “will in the main have to accept their deficits”; the Committee
  of Twenty (Rome, January 1974): countries not to “adopt policies which would merely aggravate the problems of other
  countries”; estimates of the extra cost for 1974 up to $60 billion; Japan's oil imports in March 1974 283% above a
  year earlier.
- **Price discrepancy**: the BIS has $5.11 and $11.65; the Bank of Japan's history (p. 425) $3.01 → $5.12; FRUS d271's
  note quotes Kissinger's memoir for $5.12 and gives the Tehran price as $11.60. Say which source each number comes
  from, or round (“约 5 美元”, “约 11.6 美元”) with the discrepancy in a footnote.
- **boj1986b** p. 425: on 16 October the six Gulf members of OPEC raised the posted price of Arabian Light from $3.01
  to $5.12 (70%); on 17 October OAPEC decided on 5% monthly cuts from the September level; OAPEC produced about 32% of
  world output at the end of 1972 and 55% of world exports; from 20 October a total embargo on the United States and
  the Netherlands; 4 November: November output 25% below September, a further 5% in December; Arabian Oil and Abu
  Dhabi Oil (Japanese companies) told to cut 25%.
- **frus1973oil d271** (Helms, Tehran, 26 December): “the doubling of Persian Gulf crude oil prices”; the Shah: “I cannot get
  less for my oil than other countries”; the price to be related to the cost of alternative energy ($8–$14 a barrel,
  note 2); “India's five-year economic plan will go into the trash barrel”; the non-Communist world “in a fair way to
  being shaken to its economic foundations”. Note 2: Amouzegar announced for OAPEC [the note's wording; check] a
  posted price of $11.60 for Arabian Light, about $7 a barrel for the governments; note 4: the Saudis said they had
  “strongly opposed” the increase in Tehran; Akins: “Tehran was a catastrophe and Kuwait was an insult”.
- **frus1973oil d273** (28 December): OAPEC's decisions of 25 December raised production 10% for “friendly countries” and
  kept the embargo on the United States; Kissinger on the “drastic and unjustifiable price increases announced in
  Tehran on December 23”, their “disastrously destabilizing effect on the free world's economic and monetary system”.
- **frus1973oil d277** (CIA, January 1974): a $70 billion increase in the Free World oil bill in 1974 (Western Europe
  about $33 billion, Japan $11 billion, the United States almost $16 billion); a loss of purchasing power “equivalent
  to about 3% of GNP in Western Europe and Japan”; “Unless expansionary measures are taken, all face severely
  reduced rates of economic growth”, but governments “will be cautious in inflating demand” because inflation is
  already high; producers' revenues about $95 billion, three and a half times 1973; the receipts of Saudi Arabia,
  Kuwait and the small Gulf states “will far exceed their spending capability”. This is the accommodate-or-not
  dilemma in a contemporary document.
- **hamilton2011**: production of the Arab members of OPEC in November 1973 was 4.4 mb/d below September, “a shortfall
  corresponding to 7.5% of global output”; on 1 January 1974 the Gulf countries doubled the price. Iranian output fell
  4.8 mb/d (about 7% of world production) between October 1978 and January 1979. Table 1: price increases of 51%
  (November 1973–February 1974) and 57% (May 1979–January 1980); all but one of the 11 postwar US recessions were
  associated with an oil price increase (1960 the exception) and all but one of the 12 episodes were followed by a
  recession (2003 the exception).
- **frus1973oil d237, d244**: Nixon's Project Independence (7 November 1973) and the measures of 25 November (gas stations
  closed from 9 p.m. Saturday to midnight Sunday, 68°F indoors); Schlesinger's remarks on force (d244). Use at most
  a sentence; the chapter is about macroeconomics.

### Japan

- **boj1986b** p. 432: on 21 December 1973 the Policy Board raised the discount rate by 2 points, from 7% to 9%
  (from 22 December), and reserve ratios (from 1 January 1974); 9% was a level not seen since Meiji 19, and the rate
  had never been raised 2 points at once; Fukuda, the new Finance Minister, supported tightening.
- p. 437 note 8 (read on the page image): 福田赳夫 at a press conference in Osaka after a meeting with the Kansai
  business community on 12 January 1974 said prices were 「まさに狂乱状態だ」 (『朝日新聞』 evening edition of the same
  day); the word 狂乱物価 then came into general use. Note 7: the run on 豊川信用金庫, 14 December 1973.
- p. 438: wholesale prices in February 1974 37.0% above a year earlier; the 1974 average 31.3%, the highest since
  1952; consumer prices 21.9% over 1974 and 24.5% on average; real GNE in January–March 1974 3.1% below the previous
  quarter (13% annualized); real GNE in 1974 1.2% lower, Japan's first negative growth since 1947 (昭和22年以降初めて).
- p. 440: the unemployed rose from 600–700 thousand to about a million in 1975; the increase in the crude import bill
  in 1974, $12.4 billion, about 3% of GNE (the transfer of purchasing power to the producers); the propensity to
  consume fell about 4 points; the 1974 spring wage round raised base pay 32.9%, summer bonuses 47%; 総需要抑制政策.
- p. 444: on 15 April 1975 the discount rate was cut from 9% to 8.5%. p. 451: 1975 real GNE +2.4%; consumer prices
  7.6% over the year.
- The second shock: p. 496: OPEC at Abu Dhabi (16–17 December 1978) raised Arabian Light from $12.70 by 10% on average
  over 1979 (5% in January, 14.5% by the last step); end-1981 the marker was $34, about 2.7 times; Japan's crude
  import price rose about 4 times in the first shock and about 2.8 times in the second; the increase in the oil import
  bill relative to nominal GNE was slightly larger in the second shock, and Japan's the highest of the major
  countries. p. 497: in January 1979 money growth of about 12% was judged “not low”; on 28 January Governor 森永貞一郎
  announced no further easing; a 予防的 stance; in February a comparison with the excess liquidity of 1971–1972 (money
  growth then above 20%, above 26% from spring 1972). p. 498: the Bank's “strong reflection” (強い反省) that in the
  first oil crisis its judgment had come too late (遅きに失し) and policy had lagged, so this time it had to act early;
  from late February 1979 it began talking to the government. p. 499: on 20 March 1979 Governor Morinaga told the
  Lower House finance committee that policy had moved from 「緩和基調の維持」 to 「警戒的な中立姿勢」; the government
  and business were reluctant to tighten; the Bank's maxim 「火は燃え上がらないうちに消せ」. p. 500: OPEC brought the
  $14.55 marker forward to 1 April; the Bundesbank raised its discount rate on 29 March; in early April the Bank
  resolved on an early rise and Morinaga took it to Prime Minister Ohira, citing the bitter experience of the late turn

  chairman's statement 「インフレを未然に防止し、息の長い景気上昇を図る趣旨から」; the OPEC meeting at Geneva of 26–28
  June 1979 raised the marker from $14.55 to $18 and allowed a surcharge of up to $2; the July 1979 rise follows.
  The OCR of pp. 503–533 was not finished: read the rest yourself (the later rises of 1979–1980, the 1980 wage
  round and the outcome are there), and confirm every number above on the page image (the OCR of this scan is
  noisy).
- Ch 42's row: Krugman's 3% a year per head in 1973–1992 against 8.9% in the decade before: keep it as Ch 42 states it.

### The second shock and the policy lesson

- **bis1980** p. 3: an oil price rise “which in real terms has been of the same order of magnitude as that of
  1973-74”; the dual effect: it raises prices and “levies a tax on the oil-importing countries”; the dilemma (fight
  inflation by restraining demand, or sustain demand and fuel inflation); p. 5: in 1973–74 the main industrial
  countries “were far from unanimous in assigning priority to the fight against inflation: in fact, the majority of
  them opted, for a while at least, in favour of supporting domestic demand”; now restrictive money in all, “even in
  the United States”; one-third of the counterpart deficits in Germany, Japan and the US (1974: the UK and Italy).
  p. 16: the Saudi marker crude $13.34 at the start of 1979, $26 at the start of 1980; North African and Iranian oil
  $30–34; spot prices in the second half of 1979 “fully 50 per cent. higher” than Saudi contract prices; “the
  painful decision of how much to accommodate and how much to resist”; “Accommodation could limit recessionary
  tendencies but would risk a permanent rise in inflation. Resistance could limit inflation but would risk a downturn
  in economic activity.” The chapter's compare box 迁就 vs 不迁就 rests here and on Gordon (1975).
- **gordon1975** p. 183: food (“through natural causes”) and oil (“through unnatural causes”); monetarists' advice
  (Meltzer, Friedman's Newsweek columns) against Okun's; p. 184: the four temporary “bubbles”; a one-shot price rise
  only temporarily raises inflation. pp. 200–202, the summary: with flexible prices no problem; with rigid nonfarm
  prices and fixed nominal income, a multiplier effect and a recession; the optimal policy then an increase in nominal
  income that accommodates; with wages that respond to external prices both inflation and recession are worse; with a
  permanent supply cut (“an unbreakable oil cartel”) accommodation permanently raises the inflation rate; wage
  indexation makes things worse. The general discussion: Wachter's three shocks in a row, “a succession of such
  decisions accommodates inflation” (the discussant's point as summarized, not Gordon's).
- **Friedman (1976)**, Ch 44's `friedman1976nobel`: the oil crisis cannot wholly explain the rise of inflation and
  unemployment (FORWARD_REFS row from Ch 44): reuse Ch 44's citation and page.

  1973); its cash surplus about $55 billion; the OECD area's current account from a $2 billion surplus to a deficit

  table of trade balances and net oil expenditure. p. 84: US banks' short-term liabilities to foreigners +$15.7
  billion and loans to foreigners +$17.7 billion in 1974, “a major part in the recycling of funds from surplus to

### Producers: the Dutch disease and the resource curse

- **cordenneary1982** (paraphrase only): a small open economy with two traded goods at world prices (energy and
  manufactures) and one non-traded good (services, whose price clears its market); the real exchange rate defined as
  the relative price of non-traded to traded goods; a boom (a Hicks-neutral technical improvement in energy, among
