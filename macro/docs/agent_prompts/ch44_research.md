## 2. Facts read (confirm each on the document)

- friedman1968, pp. 7–11: the natural rate “is the level that would be ground out by the Walrasian system of
  general equilibrium equations, provided there is imbedded in them the actual structural characteristics of
  the labor and commodity markets …”; Phillips's “basic defect” was “the failure to distinguish between
  nominal wages and real wages”, as Wicksell failed to separate nominal and real interest rates; Brazil
  (anticipated inflation over 75% a year, brought down to about 45%, “a sharp initial rise in unemployment”);
  “natural” not “immutable”: minimum wages, the Walsh-Healey and Davis-Bacon Acts and unions raise it; “I
  use the term ‘natural’ for the same reason Wicksell did”; the worked case: a target of 3% unemployment
  below the natural rate needs accelerating inflation; the authority “cannot know what the ‘natural’ rate
  is”. Footnote 5: a Phillips curve is stable only for a period when the anticipated rate of price change is
  stable; curves for different periods differ in level.
- friedman1976 (printed pp. 270–277): three stages (the negatively sloped curve; the natural rate
  hypothesis, “we [in particular, E. S. Phelps and myself …]”; a positively sloped curve?); the term
  natural rate “a term I introduced to parallel Knut Wicksell's ‘natural rate of interest’”, depending on
  “real” factors; reasons he gives for a rising US natural rate (more women, teenagers and part-time workers;
  more generous unemployment insurance); Japan and the UK both had low unemployment in 1950–1970 (“Japan
  experienced rapid growth, the U.K., stagnation”); “stagflation” as an “unlovely label”.
  **Table 1 (printed p. 276, PDF p. 10, read on the page image):** inflation DP (CPI, compounded from calendar
  year 1955 to 1960, etc.) and unemployment U (average of the five calendar years), seven countries:
  | | France | Germany | Italy | Japan | Sweden | UK | US | unweighted average |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1956–60 | 5.6 / 1.1 | 1.8 / 2.9 | 1.9 / 6.7 | 1.9 / 1.4 | 3.7 / 1.9 | 2.6 / 1.5 | 2.0 / 5.2 | 2.8 / 3.0 |

  (each cell DP / U, percent). Friedman's note: “DP is dated one-half year prior to associated U”. His reading:
  the averages move in opposite directions between the first two quinquennia and in the same direction
  thereafter; “these data are at most suggestive”; seven countries are not seven independent bodies of data;
  the oil crisis hit all seven.
- phelps2006: his own account of how his 1967 *Economica* and 1968 *JPE* papers differ from Friedman (1968):
  the two are “often taken to be essentially identical … simultaneous discoveries”, but “Friedman's is a model
  of the natural rate of labor force participation while mine is a model of the natural rate of unemployment”
  (confirmed 2026-11-12 on PDF p. 9, under the run-in heading “Relation to Friedman’s 1968 model.”: the two “are then treated as simultaneous discoveries of the same thing. In fact they represent discovery of two distinct phenomena.”; his example: in Friedman's model an unperceived rise in demand is “an unwelcome deviation from competitive equilibrium”, in his it moderates involuntary unemployment; a contested-question candidate: who found the vertical curve, and was it the same idea).
- mp1975: the “noninflationary rate of unemployment (NIRU)”, implied by both the “vertical” and the
  “nonvertical” schools; for 1953–71 data consistent with a stable NIRU; “NIRUA”, the NIRU for an
  unemployment rate adjusted to the labour-force composition of 1956 (the Council of Economic Advisers'
  weights), “is at most 5 percent” (the inference from their figure 1, years with unemployment above 5% all but 1962 showing falling inflation; read 2026-11-12 in the text layer, which runs words together: confirm the printed page on the image); the summary on printed p. 142: NIRU defined as “a rate such that, as long as unemployment is above it, inflation can be expected to decline-except perhaps from an initially low rate”, data for 1953–71 “consistent with the hypothesis of a well-defined NIRU, stable over time” once unemployment is adjusted for the labour force's composition, NIRU on the official rate “currently estimated at somewhat over 5½ percent” (the text layer prints “51/2”), and “a conservative interim unemployment target for mid-1977 is 6 percent”; a change in the labour
  force's composition and in the terms of trade would raise it. The origin of the later “NAIRU” (the
  compare box 自然失业率 vs NAIRU).
